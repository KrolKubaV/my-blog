**Day 1**

<Problem>

There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m, n)$ and $\dfrac{\operatorname{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so.

**(a)** Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$.

**(b)** Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

</Problem>

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

<Theorem>
Let $S$ be the multiset of all numbers on the board at any point. For a fixed prime $p$ and an integer $x$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorisation of $x$ (with $v_p(1)=0$). Consider the multiset

$$
E_p = \{v_p(x) : x \in S\}.
$$

When we replace $m,n$ with $g=\gcd(m,n)$ and $h=\dfrac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$, we have for each prime $p$:

$$
v_p(g) = \min(a,b), \qquad v_p(h) = |a-b|,
$$

where $a=v_p(m)$ and $b=v_p(n)$.
</Theorem>

<Definition>
For a fixed prime $p$, the operation replaces the two exponents $a,b$ with $\min(a,b)$ and $|a-b|$. This is exactly one step of the Euclidean algorithm applied to the pair $(a,b)$.
</Definition>

<Remark>
The sum of all $p$-exponents on the board strictly decreases in every move that affects the exponents of $p$, because

$$
\min(a,b) + |a-b| = \max(a,b) < a+b
$$

unless one of $a,b$ is zero.
</Remark>

Since the sum of exponents for each prime is a positive integer that strictly decreases whenever a move involves two numbers both divisible by $p$, the process must terminate. At termination, for each prime $p$, at most one number on the board has $v_p > 0$.

Hence, when no further moves are possible, exactly one integer $M$ on the board exceeds $1$. The value $M$ is the product over all primes $p$ of $p^{\,d_p}$, where $d_p$ is the greatest common divisor of all initial $p$-exponents — i.e. $M = \gcd$ of all original numbers. This is independent of Confucius's choices.

</details>
